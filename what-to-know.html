What to Know: The Short Version

Medical disclaimer: This private guide is general education compiled from published sources and the public website of Dr. Rovner's practice. It is not medical advice and does not replace the instructions Sarah gets from her surgical team. If anything here differs from what they say, follow them. For trouble breathing or rapidly increasing neck swelling, call 911 (see red-flag card).

The short version. Each point links to its source if you want to check it.

What the surgery is

  • The damaged disc pressing on a nerve is removed and replaced with a small moving implant, working from the front of the neck [25, 27].
  • Unlike fusion, the treated level keeps moving. In one 10-year trial, the treated level still had about 8.7° of motion vs 0.6° after fusion [47].
  • It takes about 2–3 hours under general anesthesia. Many people go home the same day or after 1–2 nights [25, 73].

What recovery usually feels like

  • A sore throat and some trouble swallowing for days to a couple of weeks are common [25, 77]. Hoarseness can happen and usually passes [27, 77].
  • Pain at the back of the neck and between the shoulder blades is common [93, 94].
  • Arm pain often improves first. Numbness is often the last thing to improve [94].
  • Walking is encouraged right away [76, 27]. Many people are back to desk work within 1–2 weeks [25]. Full recovery can take up to 6 months [29].

How it compares with fusion

  • Pooled trials found fewer repeat operations at the neighboring levels after disc replacement (4.3% vs 10.8% at 7 years for one level). The difference appeared around year 5 [52].
  • Average symptom scores were slightly better, but the difference was small enough that patients may not notice it [54].
  • Trouble swallowing was a bit less common after disc replacement (9.5% vs 12.1%) [58].

The main risks

  • Front-of-neck surgery risks include swallowing and voice problems, bleeding, infection, nerve injury and, rarely, breathing problems [27, 29].
  • Over time, bone can form around the implant (heterotopic ossification), and some designs have had bone-loss (osteolysis) problems [61, 20]. See Risks.

Three things to remember

  1. Any trouble breathing or fast-growing neck swelling means call 911 [92, 93].
  2. Follow Dr. Rovner's team's instructions over anything general.
  3. Walk, rest, eat soft foods, and ask for help.
