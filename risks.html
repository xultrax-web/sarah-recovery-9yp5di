Risks and Complications

Medical disclaimer: This private guide is general education compiled from published sources and the public website of Dr. Rovner's practice. It is not medical advice and does not replace the instructions Sarah gets from her surgical team. If anything here differs from what they say, follow them. For trouble breathing or rapidly increasing neck swelling, call 911 (see red-flag card).

Disc replacement shares the general risks of any neck surgery done from the front, and adds implant-specific risks such as heterotopic ossification, wear and osteolysis. The rates below come from specific studies and vary widely with device, follow-up length and how carefully problems were looked for.

Risks of any anterior cervical surgery

  • Any cervical spine surgery: infection, bleeding, nerve injury, spinal cord injury, reaction to anesthesia, a dural tear (tear of the sac around the nerves), failure to relieve symptoms, needing more surgery later, and rarely life-threatening problems such as heart attack, lung complications or stroke [27].
  • Front-of-neck (anterior) approach: soreness or difficulty swallowing, voice changes, breathing difficulty, injury to the esophagus, and misplaced, broken or loosened implants [27]. Cleveland Clinic adds a sore throat that comes and goes [29].
  • Disc-replacement-specific, long term: adjacent segment degeneration, artificial-disc-related failures, heterotopic ossification, and possibly a future operation [29].

Heterotopic ossification (HO)

HO is bone forming in soft tissue around the implant. Advanced HO can bridge the level and stop it moving, effectively a spontaneous fusion [61, 62].

  • Pooled prevalence: 44.6% at 12 months and 58.2% at 24 months; advanced (grade III–IV) HO 11.1% and 16.7% [61].
  • Prestige LP two-level, 10 years: grade IV HO 8.2% (upper level) and 10.3% (lower level). Grade III–IV was 42.4% at 7 years and 39.0% at 10 years [44].
  • Two-level Mobi-C at 24 months: clinically relevant HO (grade III/IV) at 11.4% of upper and 10.1% of lower levels [11].
  • Range across studies: ~7.3% to 69.2% [66].
  • Does it matter? Having any HO was not linked to worse pain or disability scores, but high-grade HO was linked to worse pain [62]. HO was associated with adjacent-segment degeneration and, perhaps surprisingly, with less dysphagia [63].
  • Risk factor: exposed endplate (an implant footprint that doesn't cover the bone) was a significant risk factor for HO [64].

Osteolysis (bone loss around the implant)

  • A systematic review found asymptomatic osteolysis in 8–64% of patients (44–64% excluding one outlier study). Severe bone loss exposing the implant occurred in under 4%. Most cases were mild and did not need revision [65].
  • Symptomatic osteolysis often required implant removal and conversion to fusion [65].
  • Device-specific signals: M6-C (34% revised at a single center; UK alert recommends annual X-rays) and Simplify (MAUDE reports of PEEK wear and osteolysis). See safety alerts [67, 20, 69, 70].

Repeat surgery

  • Index-level reoperation by 7 years: 5.2% ADR vs 12.7% ACDF (one level), 4.2% vs 13.5% (two levels) [52].
  • Implant failure ~3.3–3.7% (review) [66].
  • Revising or removing a cervical artificial disc has its own billing codes (CPT 22861 and 22864) [22].

Adjacent segment degeneration and disease

Losing motion at a fused level may speed up wear at the neighboring levels. Keeping motion with disc replacement is meant to prevent this [54, 52]. Radiographic adjacent-segment degeneration is common after disc replacement too: it was 36.0% pooled at long-term follow-up in one meta-analysis [63]. What matters most to patients is whether another operation is needed. Those rates are lower after disc replacement in pooled trials, with the difference showing at around 5 years [52, 53].

When to call your surgeon

Cleveland Clinic advises contacting your provider for continuous bleeding from the incision, yellow or green drainage, severe pain, fever, worsening or persistent difficulty swallowing or eating, or new weakness or numbness [29]. Breathing difficulty is a recognized risk of anterior neck surgery [27]. Ask your surgeon which symptoms need emergency care.
