Conditions Treated with Cervical Disc Replacement

📚 Deeper reading. This is one of the detailed research pages. You don't need it to get ready. Start with Sarah's home page and What to know.

Medical disclaimer: This private guide is general education compiled from published sources and the public website of Dr. Rovner's practice. It is not medical advice and does not replace the instructions Sarah gets from her surgical team. If anything here differs from what they say, follow them. For trouble breathing or rapidly increasing neck swelling, call 911 (see red-flag card).

FDA labels for cervical discs cover symptomatic disc disease at C3–C7 causing radiculopathy and/or myelopathy. That includes the C5–C6 and C6–C7 levels this guide focuses on [9, 18, 15].

Cervical disc herniation

A disc herniates when its jelly-like center pushes through the outer ring. If it bulges toward the spinal canal, it can press on a nerve root or the cord [26]. In younger people radiculopathy is most often caused by a herniated disc, sometimes after a sudden injury, though often there is no clear trigger [26]. AAOS lists "no significant facet joint disease or bony compression on spinal nerves" among the traits of good disc-replacement candidates, which is why a soft disc herniation is a common reason for the operation [25].

Cervical spondylosis (degenerative disc disease)

With age, discs dry out, lose height and bulge, and bone spurs form [26]. In the Rochester population study, spondylosis, disc protrusion or both were responsible for 68.4% of radiculopathy cases, and a confirmed disc protrusion alone for 21.9% [35]. Very advanced spondylosis is a reason not to use disc replacement: bridging bone spurs, disc height under 3 mm, or no remaining motion [23, 18].

Cervical radiculopathy

Radiculopathy, a "pinched nerve", is compression or irritation of a nerve root as it branches from the spinal cord. It causes pain radiating into the shoulder or arm, numbness, and weakness [26]. It is the main indication in FDA labels and coverage policies [9, 23, 21].

  • Recurrence is common: 31.7% had another episode during follow-up in the population study [35], and AAOS notes it usually settles again without specific treatment [26].
  • Substantial improvement often occurs within 4–6 months, according to a systematic review of the clinical course [36].

Degenerative cervical myelopathy

When disc bulges, bone spurs or thickened ligaments narrow the canal enough to squeeze the spinal cord, the result is myelopathy [28]. The AO Spine guideline recommends surgery for moderate and severe myelopathy. For mild myelopathy it suggests either surgery or a supervised trial of structured rehabilitation [38].

Several devices are FDA-labeled for myelopathy as well as radiculopathy [9, 18]. Expert opinion is more cautious about disc replacement for myelopathy in some groups: in a 2025 surgeon consensus on high-impact athletes, 74% agreed disc replacement was acceptable for radiculopathy, but only 47% for myelopathy [72]. NASS lists ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) as a contraindication [23].

Isolated neck pain

Neck pain without nerve or cord symptoms is not a supported indication. NASS does not recommend disc replacement for axial neck pain alone, and the FDA trials excluded it [23, 11].

Conditions that are generally not treated with ADR

  • Instability, deformity, or vertebrae compromised by trauma [23, 21, 18]
  • Infection or malignancy [23]
  • Rheumatoid arthritis or ankylosing spondylitis [23, 18]
  • More than two levels [23, 21, 22]
