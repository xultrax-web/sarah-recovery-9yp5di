Long-Term Evidence and Where Sources Disagree

📚 Deeper reading. This is one of the detailed research pages. You don't need it to get ready. Start with Sarah's home page and What to know.

Medical disclaimer: This private guide is general education compiled from published sources and the public website of Dr. Rovner's practice. It is not medical advice and does not replace the instructions Sarah gets from her surgical team. If anything here differs from what they say, follow them. For trouble breathing or rapidly increasing neck swelling, call 911 (see red-flag card).

Cervical disc replacement now has randomized trials with 7–10 years of follow-up and several meta-analyses. The consistent finding is fewer repeat operations than fusion, with symptom benefits that are similar or modestly better. The caveats are industry funding, losses to follow-up, and device-specific problems.

Meta-analyses

  • Adjacent-level surgery (11 RCTs): one level 2.3% vs 3.6% at 2 years, then 4.3% vs 10.8% at 7 years. Two levels 1.7% vs 3.4% at 2 years, then 5.1% vs 10.0% at 7 years. The divergence appears around year 5 [52].
  • Index-level reoperation: 5.2% vs 12.7% (one level) and 4.2% vs 13.5% (two levels) to 7 years [52].
  • Level-matched (16 studies, mean 7 years): adjacent-segment reoperation OR 0.45 (one level), OR 0.51 (two levels). All-cause reoperation OR 0.58 and OR 0.48 [53].
  • 10-year outcomes (6 studies): fewer secondary surgeries and adverse events with ADR. NDI and VAS were statistically but not clinically better, JOA scores were lower, and neurological success did not differ [54].
  • Two-level (8 studies, 1155 patients; only 2 RCTs): overall success OR 2.71, secondary surgery OR 0.254, complications OR 0.548, all favoring ADR [55].
  • 14 RCTs, 3160 patients: ADR as effective as ACDF and superior for some outcomes. Continuing uncertainty about prosthesis degeneration [56].
  • Real-world claims data (433,660 patients): ADR use rose 654% from 2011 to 2019. After matching, complications were 8.77% vs 12.20%, and revision surgery 3.35% vs 4.96% [60].

How much better do patients feel?

Both operations relieve arm pain and disability for most patients [42, 45]. Differences in symptom scores at 10 years are statistically significant but smaller than the minimal clinically important difference [54]. AAOS notes disc replacement "improves but does not completely eliminate pain" [25]. The clearest difference is fewer repeat operations [52, 53].

Where sources disagree

  • Mobi-C 7-year paper: two-level ACDF success is 34.2% in the abstract and 34.6% (27/78) in the results text of the same paper [42].
  • Adjacent-level surgery, single trial vs pooled data: the Bryan trial alone showed a non-significant trend (9.7% vs 15.8%, p=0.146) [47]. Pooling Bryan with Prestige ST data (6.9% vs 11.7%, p=0.023) and the meta-analyses showed significant differences [48, 52, 53].
  • M6-C: the 5-year IDE study reported low subsequent-surgery rates (3.1%) [51], whereas an independent single-center series reported 34% revisions for osteolysis at about 67 months [67]. The UK regulator now recommends annual X-rays [20].
  • Foraminotomy vs ADR: one meta-analysis found MI-PCF had more reoperations but ADR had more complications, and still recommended MI-PCF. It called its findings preliminary [59].
  • Candidacy thresholds: instability (3.0 / 3 / 3.5 mm), bone density (T −1.5 / −1.0 / any osteopenia) and hybrid constructs differ between the label, NASS, Aetna and Medicare (details) [18, 23, 22, 21].
  • Dysphagia rates range from 2.98% in claims data, to 9.46% in pooled RCTs, to "as high as ~70%" in a review, depending on definition and data source [60, 58, 66].
  • HO and swallowing: one meta-analysis found HO linked to less dysphagia but more adjacent-segment degeneration [63].
  • Materials: AAOS lists polyethylene, cobalt-chromium and titanium; Cleveland Clinic lists cobalt, titanium and stainless steel; newer devices use PEEK and ceramic [25, 29, 18].
  • Approval history: AAOS says artificial disc replacement "initially gained FDA approval for use in the U.S. in 2004" [25]. The earliest cervical approval in the FDA PMA database is Prestige (ST) in July 2007 [1]. The 2004 date likely refers to a lumbar device.
  • Bryan approval date: Aetna's policy says the FDA approved the Bryan disc in December 2009, but the FDA PMA record shows a decision date of 05/12/2009 [22, 6].
  • Smoking: one series found similar outcomes in smokers [40], while AAOS and Cleveland Clinic advise quitting because nicotine impairs healing [25, 29].
  • Myelopathy: several device labels include myelopathy [9, 18], but expert consensus did not support ADR for myelopathy in high-impact athletes [72].

Limitations of the evidence

  • Industry sponsorship: the Mobi-C trial was sponsored by Zimmer Biomet, which helped design the study, analyze the data and review the manuscript [42]. Most IDE trials share this model.
  • Loss to follow-up: 10-year data cover a subset of the original patients, e.g. 148 of 209 and 118 of 188 [44]. The Bryan employment analysis noted a "very high rate of loss of patients to the follow-up" [49].
  • Control groups differ: randomized ACDF, historical ACDF, or another disc [41, 18, 5].
  • Selected patients: trials excluded heavy smokers, prior neck fusion, osteoporosis and more [11], so results may not apply to everyone.
