What the practice says about cervical disc replacement

  • Surgery is done "through the front part of the neck," between the muscle and the swallowing and breathing tubes Stated by practice [77].
  • Swallowing: the esophagus is gently moved aside, which "can cause a week or 2 of difficulty swallowing," and only rarely lasting problems Stated by practice [77].
  • Voice: the nerve to the vocal cord can be irritated, "usually transient" Stated by practice [77].
  • Infection, nerve injury and bleeding are described as very rare Stated by practice [77].
  • Good candidates have a damaged disc pressing on a nerve root or the spinal cord, with only mild arthritis in the side (uncovertebral) and back (facet) joints Stated by practice [77].

  2. Which implant will you use, and why that one? The practice site mentions the M6 disc, which has since been discontinued (see Which implant?) [77, 20].
  3. Is it possible you'll decide on fusion during surgery instead? If so, when would I find out?
  4. Where will it be done, and will I go home the same day or stay one night [78]?
  5. How long will surgery take? Who will update Wesley, and how?

Before surgery

  1. Which medicines and supplements do I stop, and on what date? When do I restart them [86]?
  2. When exactly do I stop eating and drinking? Are clear liquids allowed, and until when [87]?
  3. What time do I arrive, and who calls me with that time?

What to Know: The Short Version

The short version. Each point links to its source if you want to check it.

What the surgery is

  • The damaged disc pressing on a nerve is removed and replaced with a small moving implant, working from the front of the neck [25, 27].
  • Unlike fusion, the treated level keeps moving. In one 10-year trial, the treated level still had about 8.7° of motion vs 0.6° after fusion [47].
  • It takes about 2–3 hours under general anesthesia. Many people go home the same day or after 1–2 nights [25, 73].

What recovery usually feels like

  • A sore throat and some trouble swallowing for days to a couple of weeks are common [25, 77]. Hoarseness can happen and usually passes [27, 77].
  • Pain at the back of the neck and between the shoulder blades is common [93, 94].
  • Arm pain often improves first. Numbness is often the last thing to improve [94].
  • Walking is encouraged right away [76, 27]. Many people are back to desk work within 1–2 weeks [25]. Full recovery can take up to 6 months [29].

How it compares with fusion

  • Pooled trials found fewer repeat operations at the neighboring levels after disc replacement (4.3% vs 10.8% at 7 years for one level). The difference appeared around year 5 [52].
  • Average symptom scores were slightly better, but the difference was small enough that patients may not notice it [54].
  • Trouble swallowing was a bit less common after disc replacement (9.5% vs 12.1%) [58].

The main risks

  • Front-of-neck surgery risks include swallowing and voice problems, bleeding, infection, nerve injury and, rarely, breathing problems [27, 29].
  • Over time, bone can form around the implant (heterotopic ossification), and some designs have had bone-loss (osteolysis) problems [61, 20]. See Risks.

Three things to remember

  1. Any trouble breathing or fast-growing neck swelling means call 911 [92, 93].
  2. Follow Dr. Rovner's team's instructions over anything general.
  3. Walk, rest, eat soft foods, and ask for help.

Red-Flag Card: When to Call for Help

Print this card and keep it on the fridge. When in doubt, call. No one on the team minds a call. The categories below come from published instructions written for front-of-neck spine surgery. Your surgeon's instructions take priority.

Call 911 or go to the ER now

  • Any trouble breathing, or a feeling that your breathing has changed. Swallowing trouble can be normal; breathing trouble never is [92, 93, 27].
  • Neck swelling that grows quickly or is large, especially with trouble breathing [93, 94].
  • Chest pain or sudden shortness of breath [93, 91, 88].
  • Significant new weakness, or being unable to fully move your arms or legs [93, 94].
  • Loss of control of bowels or bladder [94].

Call the surgeon's office right away

  • Fever: over 101°F that persists, per the practice [76]. MedlinePlus says chills or fever of 101°F or higher [88]. Ask the team for their exact number.
  • Swallowing that is getting worse or not improving, trouble eating or drinking, or coughing when you swallow [29, 89].
  • Incision: continuous bleeding; yellow or green drainage; more redness, swelling or warmth; or the edges opening [29, 88].
  • New numbness, tingling or weakness, or a change from how you felt before surgery [29, 88, 94].

  • A sore throat for several days, and some swallowing difficulty for days to weeks, especially with dry food or big pills [92, 27, 94].

For Wesley: How to Help

Thank you for being Sarah's person through this. Your most important jobs are to keep her safe, keep her comfortable, and notice changes early. The rest is snacks and patience.

The first 72 hours

  • Drive her home and stay. A responsible adult has to take her home, and she should stay in the house for the first 24 hours [87]. One spine manual suggests having someone there at night for the first night or so [92].
  • Watch her breathing and her neck. Any breathing trouble, or neck swelling that grows quickly, means call 911 [92, 93]. Some bruising and mild swelling is expected [92].
  • Medicines on schedule. Write each dose in the daily log. The practice says narcotics cause constipation, so use the minimum, and no NSAIDs [76]. Check whether her prescription already contains acetaminophen before giving Tylenol [93].
  • Help her walk. Short, frequent walks around the house. The practice discourages bed rest [76]. Hold the stair rail and stay with her on stairs [93].
  • Throat comfort. Ice chips and popsicles help a sore throat [93]. Warm or cold drinks are both fine [92].
  • Dressing and shower. The practice says the dressing comes off on day 2–3 and she can shower once the wound is sealed (about 3 days) and dry. Be there for the first shower [76, 88].

The first two weeks

  • Do the lifting and reaching: groceries, laundry, vacuuming, high or low shelves [91]. Norton advises nothing over 10 lb until the surgeon says it's OK [93]. Dr. Rovner's practice says avoid "excessive" lifting, bending and twisting [76].
  • Driving her: she shouldn't drive while on narcotic pain medicine or before she can turn to check her blind spot [91]. Ask the surgeon before she drives. Keep car trips short at first, and stop to stretch on longer rides [88].
  • Keep an eye on the incision each day [88] and on how her swallowing is going. It should slowly get easier [29].
  • Make sure she gets to the first follow-up: To fill in: date of first follow-up.

Meals and soft foods

The practice says there are normally no diet restrictions, and that this is not a time to diet. She needs calories to heal [76]. For a sore throat or swallowing discomfort:

  • Soft, moist foods: scrambled eggs, mac and cheese, soups and stews, mashed potatoes with gravy, cooked cereal, cooked vegetables, cottage cheese, custard, pudding [90]. Add broth, gravy or sauce to moisten food [90].
  • How to eat: sit upright; small bites of less than a teaspoon; chew well and swallow before the next bite; don't talk while swallowing; stay upright for 30–45 minutes after eating [89]. Take small bites and add regular foods back as she feels better [93].
  • Drinks: thin liquids can be harder at first, so drink slowly. Thicker drinks like smoothies may be easier [92].
  • Where advice differs: one practice suggests avoiding dairy until swallowing feels normal, because it can cause mucus [92]. The VA list includes dairy, such as cottage cheese and custard [90]. The VA suggests a sip after each bite, while MedlinePlus says not to wash solids down with liquids [90, 89]. Go with whatever feels easiest for her, and ask the team if swallowing is hard.
  • Red meat and iron help with post-op anemia, according to the practice [76]. Fluids and fiber help with constipation [91, 93].

Emotional support

  • Feeling anxious before surgery is normal [92]. Recovery has good days and slow days.
  • Accept help from friends and family. Patient booklets suggest a list of jobs they can take on: meals, errands, rides, laundry, yard work [91]. One manual says a support person helps patients have a smoother recovery [92]. Point them to the Meals & Visits sign-up and post news on Recovery updates.
  • Practical tips (not from a medical source): keep visits short, protect her rest, celebrate small wins like the first shower or the first longer walk, and take breaks for yourself too.

What to watch for

Keep the red-flag card on the fridge. Breathing trouble or fast-growing neck swelling means call 911. Fever, worsening swallowing, changes in the incision, or new weakness or numbness means call the surgeon [92, 93, 29].

Know

The Operation, Step by Step

Cervical disc replacement is done through the front of the neck, the same approach as fusion (ACDF). What differs is the final step: a moving implant goes into the disc space instead of a bone graft or cage [27].

Before surgery

  • Imaging and tests: X-ray, MRI or myelogram; sometimes CT and bone-density testing [29, 25, 22].
  • Medications: your surgeon will review medicines, vitamins and supplements, and some may need to be stopped. Don't stop any medicine unless told to [25, 29].
  • Nicotine: you may be asked to stop tobacco or nicotine products at least 4 weeks before surgery [29].
  • Fasting: usually nothing to eat or drink after the night before surgery [25].
  • Ride home: someone else must drive you home [29].

Step by step

  1. Anesthesia and positioning. General anesthesia through an IV, lying on your back [25, 29].
  2. Incision. Made on the front of the neck, usually in a skin crease. Its exact location and length vary [25, 29].
  3. Anterior approach. The trachea, esophagus and blood vessels are moved aside to reach the front of the spine [29, 25].
  4. Imaging. Fluoroscopy (live X-ray) is used throughout to confirm the level and position [29, 25].
  5. Discectomy and decompression. The damaged disc is removed, along with bone spurs or fragments pressing on the nerve root or cord. CPT code 22856 explicitly includes "end plate preparation" and "osteophytectomy for nerve root or spinal cord decompression and microdissection" [22].
  6. Implant insertion. The artificial disc is sized and inserted, and its position is checked on imaging [25].
  7. Closure. Structures are moved back into place, the incision is closed and dressed [29, 25].

How long does it take?

  • AAOS: most disc replacements take 2 to 3 hours [25].
  • Two-level Mobi-C trial: mean operative time 2.1 hours (ADR) vs 1.8 hours (ACDF) [11].

Hospital stay

  • Usually 1–2 days; some people go home the same day [25].
  • In the two-level Simplify study, mean stay was 0.59 days vs 1.31 days for historical ACDF controls. FDA's summary says this probably reflects more outpatient surgery sites and is not clinically meaningful [18].
  • In the 2013 two-level Mobi-C trial, mean stay was 2.2 vs 2.4 days [11].

Right after surgery

  • You will be monitored in the recovery room as you wake up [29].
  • Temporary difficulty swallowing for a few days is common [25]. After anterior neck surgery, AAOS notes swallowing solids may be hard for a few weeks and hoarseness can occur [27].
  • Most patients are able to walk and eat on the first day after surgery [27].
  • Your surgeon may have you wear a protective collar at first, and excessive neck motion is limited while bone grows onto the implant [25].

Continue to the week-by-week recovery timeline.

Know

Recovery Week by Week: Work, Driving and Sports

Recovery after disc replacement is usually faster than after fusion because there is no fusion to wait for. Timelines still vary by person, by job, and by surgeon. Scroll to animate the timeline. Each step shows where the guidance comes from, and sources disagree slightly.

Day of surgery – day 1

Hospital or same-day discharge

Most people stay 1–2 days; some go home the same day [25]. Walking and eating usually start on the first day [27]. Someone else must drive you home [29].

Day 2

Daily self-care

Activities of daily living such as grooming and preparing meals usually resume by the second day [29]. Swallowing may feel difficult for a few days [25].

Weeks 1–2

Desk work, driving, travel

AAOS: most patients return to driving and desk jobs within 1–2 weeks [25]. An expert panel agreed patients may return to walking, social activities, sedentary work, air travel and sexual activity within 2 weeks [71]. Your surgeon decides when driving is safe for you.

Weeks 2–3

Regular light activities

Cleveland Clinic: light activities between 2 and 3 weeks [29]. Avoid heavy lifting and aerobic exercise until cleared [29].

Weeks 4–6

Light exercise, physical therapy, most activities

Light physical activity at 4–6 weeks (expert consensus) [71]. Full activities except contact sports by 4–6 weeks [29]. Range-of-motion and strengthening exercises often begin around 4–6 weeks after anterior neck surgery, sometimes with PT [27].

About 6 weeks

Training and heavier work

The expert panel agreed on intense conditioning and sport-specific training at 6 weeks, but had only weaker agreement on physically demanding work at 4–6 weeks and high-intensity sport at 6 weeks [71]. In the Bryan trial, 49.2% of disc-replacement patients were back at work at 6 weeks vs 39.4% after fusion [49].

Weeks 6–12

Strenuous jobs

AAOS: returning to strenuous jobs usually takes 6–12 weeks [25]. Mean return to work in the two-level Mobi-C trial was 45.9 days vs 66.8 days after fusion [11].

About 3 months

Contact and collision sports (selected athletes)

In a 34-surgeon consensus, the most endorsed timelines after one-level replacement were 6 weeks or 3 months for practice and 3 months for games. Only 23.5% would let an athlete return to high-impact, high-frequency sport after a two-level replacement, compared with 57.6% after one level [72].

Up to 6 months

Full recovery

It may take up to 6 months to recover fully [29]. Improvements in pain and disability come over weeks to months, and disc replacement improves but does not completely eliminate pain [25].

1 year and beyond

Follow-up imaging

Follow-up visits check that the disc is working [29]. Work outcomes: disc-replacement patients had higher odds of working at 6 weeks, 3 months and 1 year, but not at 2 years or later [57]. People with an M6-C should have annual X-rays according to the UK MHRA [20].

Return to work: the evidence

  • Meta-analysis of 16 studies (5657 patients): higher odds of working at 6 weeks (OR 1.33), 3 months (OR 1.58) and 1 year (OR 1.35); about 9.9 days earlier on average; no difference at 2 years or later [57].
  • Bryan trial: more patients back at 6 weeks, no difference by 6 months [49].

Driving

AAOS says most people can drive within 1–2 weeks [25]. Ask your surgeon when you may drive, especially if you are still taking prescription pain medicine or wearing a collar.

Sports

  • General patients: full activity except contact sports by 4–6 weeks [29]; sport-specific training at 6 weeks (expert consensus) [71].
  • High-impact athletes: much less agreement, and more caution after two-level surgery [72].

Where recovery advice differs

  • Light activities: "1–2 weeks" for desk work and driving (AAOS) vs "2–3 weeks" for regular light activities (Cleveland Clinic) [25, 29].
  • Full recovery: "full activities excluding contact sports by 4–6 weeks" vs "up to six months" for full recovery, both from Cleveland Clinic. These describe different milestones [29].
  • Strenuous work: 6–12 weeks (AAOS) vs only weak expert consensus for 4–6 weeks [25, 71].

Know

Risks and Complications

Disc replacement shares the general risks of any neck surgery done from the front, and adds implant-specific risks such as heterotopic ossification, wear and osteolysis. The rates below come from specific studies and vary widely with device, follow-up length and how carefully problems were looked for.

Risks of any anterior cervical surgery

  • Any cervical spine surgery: infection, bleeding, nerve injury, spinal cord injury, reaction to anesthesia, a dural tear (tear of the sac around the nerves), failure to relieve symptoms, needing more surgery later, and rarely life-threatening problems such as heart attack, lung complications or stroke [27].
  • Front-of-neck (anterior) approach: soreness or difficulty swallowing, voice changes, breathing difficulty, injury to the esophagus, and misplaced, broken or loosened implants [27]. Cleveland Clinic adds a sore throat that comes and goes [29].
  • Disc-replacement-specific, long term: adjacent segment degeneration, artificial-disc-related failures, heterotopic ossification, and possibly a future operation [29].

Heterotopic ossification (HO)

HO is bone forming in soft tissue around the implant. Advanced HO can bridge the level and stop it moving, effectively a spontaneous fusion [61, 62].

  • Pooled prevalence: 44.6% at 12 months and 58.2% at 24 months; advanced (grade III–IV) HO 11.1% and 16.7% [61].
  • Prestige LP two-level, 10 years: grade IV HO 8.2% (upper level) and 10.3% (lower level). Grade III–IV was 42.4% at 7 years and 39.0% at 10 years [44].
  • Two-level Mobi-C at 24 months: clinically relevant HO (grade III/IV) at 11.4% of upper and 10.1% of lower levels [11].
  • Range across studies: ~7.3% to 69.2% [66].
  • Does it matter? Having any HO was not linked to worse pain or disability scores, but high-grade HO was linked to worse pain [62]. HO was associated with adjacent-segment degeneration and, perhaps surprisingly, with less dysphagia [63].
  • Risk factor: exposed endplate (an implant footprint that doesn't cover the bone) was a significant risk factor for HO [64].

Osteolysis (bone loss around the implant)

  • A systematic review found asymptomatic osteolysis in 8–64% of patients (44–64% excluding one outlier study). Severe bone loss exposing the implant occurred in under 4%. Most cases were mild and did not need revision [65].
  • Symptomatic osteolysis often required implant removal and conversion to fusion [65].
  • Device-specific signals: M6-C (34% revised at a single center; UK alert recommends annual X-rays) and Simplify (MAUDE reports of PEEK wear and osteolysis). See safety alerts [67, 20, 69, 70].

Repeat surgery

  • Index-level reoperation by 7 years: 5.2% ADR vs 12.7% ACDF (one level), 4.2% vs 13.5% (two levels) [52].
  • Implant failure ~3.3–3.7% (review) [66].
  • Revising or removing a cervical artificial disc has its own billing codes (CPT 22861 and 22864) [22].

Adjacent segment degeneration and disease

Losing motion at a fused level may speed up wear at the neighboring levels. Keeping motion with disc replacement is meant to prevent this [54, 52]. Radiographic adjacent-segment degeneration is common after disc replacement too: it was 36.0% pooled at long-term follow-up in one meta-analysis [63]. What matters most to patients is whether another operation is needed. Those rates are lower after disc replacement in pooled trials, with the difference showing at around 5 years [52, 53].

When to call your surgeon

Cleveland Clinic advises contacting your provider for continuous bleeding from the incision, yellow or green drainage, severe pain, fever, worsening or persistent difficulty swallowing or eating, or new weakness or numbness [29]. Breathing difficulty is a recognized risk of anterior neck surgery [27]. Ask your surgeon which symptoms need emergency care.
