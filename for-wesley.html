Recovery

For Wesley: How to Help

Medical disclaimer: This private guide is general education compiled from published sources and the public website of Dr. Rovner's practice. It is not medical advice and does not replace the instructions Sarah gets from her surgical team. If anything here differs from what they say, follow them. For trouble breathing or rapidly increasing neck swelling, call 911 (see red-flag card).

Thank you for being Sarah's person through this. Your most important jobs are to keep her safe, keep her comfortable, and notice changes early. The rest is snacks and patience.

The first 72 hours

  • Drive her home and stay. A responsible adult has to take her home, and she should stay in the house for the first 24 hours [87]. One spine manual suggests having someone there at night for the first night or so [92].
  • Watch her breathing and her neck. Any breathing trouble, or neck swelling that grows quickly, means call 911 [92, 93]. Some bruising and mild swelling is expected [92].
  • Medicines on schedule. Write each dose in the daily log. The practice says narcotics cause constipation, so use the minimum, and no NSAIDs [76]. Check whether her prescription already contains acetaminophen before giving Tylenol [93].
  • Help her walk. Short, frequent walks around the house. The practice discourages bed rest [76]. Hold the stair rail and stay with her on stairs [93].
  • Throat comfort. Ice chips and popsicles help a sore throat [93]. Warm or cold drinks are both fine [92].
  • Dressing and shower. The practice says the dressing comes off on day 2–3 and she can shower once the wound is sealed (about 3 days) and dry. Be there for the first shower [76, 88].

The first two weeks

  • Do the lifting and reaching: groceries, laundry, vacuuming, high or low shelves [91]. Norton advises nothing over 10 lb until the surgeon says it's OK [93]. Dr. Rovner's practice says avoid "excessive" lifting, bending and twisting [76].
  • Driving her: she shouldn't drive while on narcotic pain medicine or before she can turn to check her blind spot [91]. Ask the surgeon before she drives. Keep car trips short at first, and stop to stretch on longer rides [88].
  • Keep an eye on the incision each day [88] and on how her swallowing is going. It should slowly get easier [29].
  • Make sure she gets to the first follow-up: To fill in: date of first follow-up.

Meals and soft foods

The practice says there are normally no diet restrictions, and that this is not a time to diet. She needs calories to heal [76]. For a sore throat or swallowing discomfort:

  • Soft, moist foods: scrambled eggs, mac and cheese, soups and stews, mashed potatoes with gravy, cooked cereal, cooked vegetables, cottage cheese, custard, pudding [90]. Add broth, gravy or sauce to moisten food [90].
  • How to eat: sit upright; small bites of less than a teaspoon; chew well and swallow before the next bite; don't talk while swallowing; stay upright for 30–45 minutes after eating [89]. Take small bites and add regular foods back as she feels better [93].
  • Drinks: thin liquids can be harder at first, so drink slowly. Thicker drinks like smoothies may be easier [92].
  • Where advice differs: one practice suggests avoiding dairy until swallowing feels normal, because it can cause mucus [92]. The VA list includes dairy, such as cottage cheese and custard [90]. The VA suggests a sip after each bite, while MedlinePlus says not to wash solids down with liquids [90, 89]. Go with whatever feels easiest for her, and ask the team if swallowing is hard.
  • Red meat and iron help with post-op anemia, according to the practice [76]. Fluids and fiber help with constipation [91, 93].

Emotional support

  • Feeling anxious before surgery is normal [92]. Recovery has good days and slow days.
  • Accept help from friends and family. Patient booklets suggest a list of jobs they can take on: meals, errands, rides, laundry, yard work [91]. One manual says a support person helps patients have a smoother recovery [92]. Point them to the Meals & Visits sign-up and post news on Recovery updates.
  • Practical tips (not from a medical source): keep visits short, protect her rest, celebrate small wins like the first shower or the first longer walk, and take breaks for yourself too.

What to watch for

Keep the red-flag card on the fridge. Breathing trouble or fast-growing neck swelling means call 911. Fever, worsening swallowing, changes in the incision, or new weakness or numbness means call the surgeon [92, 93, 29].
