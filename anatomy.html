Anatomy, Nerve Roots and Symptoms by Level

It is not medical advice and does not replace the instructions Sarah gets from her surgical team. If anything here differs from what they say, follow them. Knowing which disc presses on which nerve explains why a C5–C6 problem often shows up near the thumb and a C6–C7 problem near the middle finger. It also explains why these maps are only a rough guide.

The cervical spine in brief

The neck (cervical spine) is made of seven small vertebrae that begin at the base of the skull. Stacked together they form a canal that protects the spinal cord [26]. Nerve roots branch off the cord and exit through openings on each side called foramina [26].

Between the vertebrae are flexible discs, about half an inch thick. Each has a tough outer ring (annulus fibrosus) and a soft, jelly-like center (nucleus pulposus) [26]. With age, discs lose water and height and bulge, and the body forms bone spurs that can narrow the foramina and pinch a nerve root [26]. These changes are common even without symptoms. AAOS notes that nearly half of middle-aged and older people have worn discs that cause no pain [26], and MRI studies of people without symptoms find disc degeneration becoming more common with age [37].

Which disc affects which nerve root?

In the neck, a disc problem usually affects the nerve root numbered one higher than the upper vertebra: the C5–C6 disc affects the C6 root and the C6–C7 disc affects the C7 root. This is the convention used in surgically confirmed series [32].

Dermatomes and myotomes, animated

A dermatome is the skin area mainly supplied by one nerve root. A myotome is the group of muscles mainly powered by one root. When a herniated disc or bone spur presses on a root, pain often starts in the neck and travels down the arm in that root's territory. It is usually burning or sharp and may come with pins-and-needles [26]. Use the buttons to switch between a C6 and a C7 pattern.

How common is each level?

In a population-based study from Rochester, Minnesota, the C7 root was the most frequently involved, followed by C6 [35]. StatPearls summarizes C7 as involved in more than half of cases and C6 in about a quarter [31]. The same population study found an average annual incidence of 83.2 per 100,000 people, peaking at 202.9 per 100,000 in people aged 50–54 [35].

Why symptom maps are only a guide

  • In 227 patients whose level was confirmed by successful single-level surgery, 62.6% had a standard dermatome pattern and 67.3% a standard myotome pattern. The C6 root was the most variable [32].
  • In a study comparing MRI-confirmed C6 and C7 compression, the locations of pain and numbness were "not distinctly different" between the two. The authors advised caution when predicting the level from symptoms alone [33].
  • Your surgeon will combine your symptoms with examination (strength, sensation, reflexes) and imaging before deciding which level is responsible [26]. See Diagnosis & imaging.

Radiculopathy vs myelopathy

Radiculopathy (pinched nerve root)

  • Pain from the neck down the arm in one root's area, often burning or sharp [26]
  • Tingling or "pins and needles" in fingers or hand; numbness; weakness [26]
  • May worsen with extending or turning the neck. Some people get relief by resting a hand on top of the head ("shoulder abduction relief") [26]

Myelopathy (spinal cord compression)

  • Clumsy hands, trouble with buttons or handwriting [28]
  • Imbalance or trouble walking; weakness in arms or hands [28]
  • Usually develops slowly and progresses over time. AAOS calls it "generally a surgical condition" and warns that manipulation should never be used for spinal cord compression [28]

When to be seen promptly: new or worsening weakness, loss of hand dexterity, or problems with balance and walking can mean the spinal cord is involved. These symptoms need medical assessment rather than waiting it out [28, 38].
